Handstand: how to learn it, step by step

By João Gonzalez, certified Exercise Specialist and Personal Trainer. Updated on 8 November 2026.

Health notice. This guide is general information and does not replace an assessment by a doctor or health professional. If you feel pain, stop. If you have high blood pressure, glaucoma or another eye condition, or injuries to your shoulders, wrists or neck, talk to your doctor first, because being upside down may not be advisable.

To learn the handstand, first prepare your wrists and shoulders, then work through the back-to-wall handstand and the chest-to-wall handstand, practise toe pulls off the wall and only then the kick-up to a freestanding handstand. Short, frequent practice works better, 10 to 15 minutes, 3 to 6 times a week, than long sessions to exhaustion. How long it takes varies a lot from person to person.

Before you start

These points tell you whether your body is ready to go upside down safely:

  • You can raise your arms next to your ears without arching your lower back.
  • You can bend your wrists back about 80 to 90 degrees without pain.
  • You can hold a plank for 60 seconds.
  • You can do 3 sets of 8 pike push-ups.

The 7 stages of the handstand

Each stage has a target. Only move on when you hit it with clean technique.

  1. Wrists and pike hold

    Warm up your wrists and hold a pike: hands on the floor, hips over your hands and arms next to your ears.

    Target: 3 sets of 30 s. Move on when there is no pain and your shoulders stay open.

  2. Back-to-wall handstand

    Kick up with your back to the wall and learn the cartwheel bail to both sides.

    Target: 5 sets of 20 s. Move on when you can bail out safely to both sides.

  3. Chest-to-wall handstand

    Walk up with your chest facing the wall, hands 10 to 20 cm from it.

    Target: 5 sets of 30 s. Move on when your body stays in line, with no banana shape.

  4. Toe pulls off the wall

    Facing the wall, take one foot off, then both, and hold a few seconds unsupported.

    Target: 5 sets of 5 attempts of 3 to 5 s. Move on when you keep your balance with your fingers and palms.

  5. Kick-up to freestanding

    Practise the entry near the wall or in open space.

    Target: 10 to 15 min of practice, 5 to 10 s holds. Move on when the entry works in at least half of your attempts.

  6. Freestanding handstand

    Hold a straight line, away from the wall.

    Target: 15 to 30 s holds. Move on when you correct your balance with your hands only.

  7. Consistent freestanding handstand

    Repeat long holds with good quality.

    Target: 3 sets of 30 to 60 s. Move on when you are ready for the press, the HSPU or the one-arm handstand.

Common mistakes

  • Banana back. Arching your lower back usually means your shoulders do not open fully yet. Go back to the pike hold and the chest-to-wall handstand.
  • Not knowing how to fall. Learn the cartwheel bail before the freestanding handstand. Fear of falling holds people back more than lack of strength.
  • Hands too far from the wall. Facing the wall, keep your hands 10 to 20 cm away. Further out, your body bends.
  • Skipping stages. Each stage prepares the next one. Skipping one usually costs more time than doing it.
  • Long, rare sessions. The handstand is balance and technique. You learn it through many good entries, not sets to exhaustion.
  • Balancing with your whole body. In a freestanding handstand, corrections come from your fingers and palms, with your body tight and in line.
  • Forgetting your wrists. Always warm them up. Pain that does not go away is a sign to stop and get help.

How much to train

  • 10 to 15 minutes, 3 to 6 times a week. Short, frequent practice beats one long session a week.
  • At the start of your workout, after the warm-up, while you are fresh.
  • To move on, hit the stage target with clean technique in 2 workouts in a row and without pain.
  • Go back one stage if your technique breaks in more than half of your sets, if pain does not go away or if you go 3 weeks without progress. Also check your sleep, stress and training volume.
  • Before each attempt, picture your body line and the entry. I use this principle for every skill.

How long does it take? There is no fixed timeline. The first stages often take a few weeks each and the last ones can take months. It depends on your starting point, your consistency and your recovery.

Strength for the handstand

Alongside this, shoulder strength helps. The order I use is pike push-up, pike push-up with feet elevated, wall HSPU negatives, partial HSPU and, finally, full HSPU (3 sets of 5).
